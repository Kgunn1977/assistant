# Humanize Text

Take a piece of text and rewrite it so it reads like it was written by a careful human, not generated by a chatbot. The goal is not to "trick a detector" with gimmicks (those break the prose) — it's to remove the genuine stylistic and structural habits that make LLM output recognizable, while keeping the author's meaning, facts, and voice intact.

This skill is built from the patterns catalogued at Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing. The full catalogue with definitions and example word lists lives in `references/ai-tells.md` and `references/word-lists.md` — read those when you need detail on a specific category. The SKILL.md below is enough for most rewrites.

## Core principle

LLM text is recognizable because it "regresses to the mean": it reaches for the most statistically average phrasing, inflates significance, hedges with vague authority, and decorates everything with a fixed set of vocabulary and rhythmic tics. Humanizing means doing the opposite — being specific, plain, and uneven. Real writing has texture: varied sentence length, the occasional blunt short sentence, concrete detail, and no compulsion to editorialize about why something "matters."

Crucially: **do not just swap out flagged words for synonyms.** That leaves the underlying structure intact and can make detection harder rather than producing honest prose. Fix the deeper habit — the inflation, the formula, the hedge — not just its surface vocabulary.

## Workflow

1. **Read the text and identify which tells are present.** Run through the checklist below. Most AI text has a cluster of these, not just one.
2. **Rewrite to remove them**, preserving meaning, facts, citations, and the author's intent. When unsure whether a claim is the author's or an AI embellishment, keep the factual core and cut the editorializing.
3. **Preserve the author's voice and register.** If the original is a casual blog post, don't make it sterile; if it's technical, stay technical. Humanizing is not flattening.
4. **Re-read your output as if hunting for AI tells.** It's easy to introduce new ones (e.g. rule-of-three, an em-dash flourish) while fixing old ones. Do a second pass.
5. **Report what you changed** briefly, so the user understands the edits — unless they asked for just the clean text.

If the user pasted text with no other instruction, default to returning the rewritten text first, then a short note on the main categories of tells you removed.

## The checklist

### Content & framing (the deepest tells — fix these first)

- **Inflated significance / "importance" padding.** LLMs constantly tell the reader how important, notable, enduring, or influential something is. Cut sentences whose only job is to assert significance ("stands as a testament to", "played a pivotal role", "left an indelible mark", "rich cultural heritage"). State the fact; let it carry its own weight.
- **Superficial analysis tacked on.** Watch for trailing present-participle ("-ing") clauses that add hollow commentary: "..., highlighting its importance", "..., reflecting broader trends", "..., cementing its legacy". Delete them or replace with a real, specific observation.
- **Promotional / travel-brochure tone.** Even on neutral topics, LLMs drift into ad copy ("nestled in the heart of", "vibrant", "boasts", "must-visit"). Rewrite to neutral, factual description.
- **Vague attribution / weasel words.** "Observers note", "experts argue", "it is widely regarded", "some critics say" — with no actual source. Either name a real source or cut the claim. Don't attribute opinions to a faceless authority.
- **Canned proof-of-notability.** Listing what kinds of outlets covered something ("received coverage in regional and national media", "profiled in industry publications") as a way of insisting it matters. Cut it or replace with the specific fact.
- **Outline-shaped conclusions.** Formulaic "Despite its challenges... Future prospects..." / "Challenges and Legacy" endings. Drop the rigid scaffold; end where the content naturally ends.
- **Prefer the specific over the generic.** The single biggest humanizing move: replace "a revolutionary titan of industry" with "inventor of the first train-coupling device." Concrete, slightly surprising, true detail is the opposite of AI text.

### Language & grammar

- **AI-vocabulary density.** A cluster of the signature words (delve, underscore, tapestry, intricate, pivotal, crucial, robust, showcase, testament, foster, leverage, garner, multifaceted, "it's worth noting", "in today's world") is one of the strongest tells. Replace with plain equivalents — and reduce the count, don't just substitute. See `references/word-lists.md` for the full list and the era-by-era breakdown.
- **Restore plain "is/are".** LLMs avoid simple copulas, preferring "serves as a", "stands as", "represents", "features", "offers", "boasts". Change "The building serves as the headquarters" to "The building is the headquarters." Use "has" instead of "features/offers/boasts" where it just means has.
- **Negative parallelisms.** "Not just X, but Y", "It's not X, it's Y", "no X, no Y, just Z". Distinctive and overused — rewrite as a plain statement.
- **Rule of three.** Compulsive triples ("fast, reliable, and scalable"; "adjective, adjective, adjective"). Break the pattern: use one or two items, or vary the structure.
- **Elegant variation / forced synonyms.** LLMs avoid repeating a word even when repetition is natural, cycling through synonyms for the same thing. It's fine — often better — to just repeat the plain word.
- **Even rhythm.** AI sentences tend toward uniform medium length. Add variation: a short, blunt sentence next to a longer one. Vary sentence openings.

### Style & formatting

- **Title Case headings** to sentence case unless the house style genuinely requires title case.
- **Overuse of boldface** for emphasis, especially "key takeaways" style bolding of a word every few lines — remove most of it.
- **Inline-header bullet lists** ("**Term:** description" repeated down a list) — convert to prose, or to plain bullets, where a list isn't truly warranted.
- **Em-dash overuse** — LLMs lean on em dashes for punched-up clauses far more than ordinary writers. Replace many with commas, parentheses, periods, or colons. Keep a few if they're genuinely the right call.
- **Curly quotes / apostrophes** appearing in contexts where straight quotes are expected (code, plain text, many web forms) — normalize to straight quotes if that matches the target medium. (Note: curly quotes are correct in professionally typeset prose — judge by destination.)
- **Unnecessary tables** that should be a sentence or two — convert to prose.
- **Skipped heading levels** and **horizontal rules before every heading** — fix to normal document structure.

### Consistency

- **No abrupt style shifts.** If only part of a document was AI-generated, it often reads as a tonal seam against the rest. Make the whole piece consistent in register and rhythm.

## Quick before/after patterns

**Example 1 — significance padding + AI vocabulary**
Input: "Founded in 1923, the company stands as a testament to enduring craftsmanship, playing a pivotal role in shaping the region's vibrant manufacturing landscape."
Output: "The company was founded in 1923 and makes furniture. It was one of the region's larger employers through the 1960s."

**Example 2 — copula avoidance + trailing analysis**
Input: "The museum serves as a cultural hub for the city, offering a diverse array of exhibits that reflect its rich history, underscoring its importance to the community."
Output: "The museum is in the city center and holds about 4,000 objects, mostly local industrial history."

**Example 3 — negative parallelism + rule of three**
Input: "This isn't just a phone — it's a statement. Sleek, powerful, and intuitive, it redefines what a device can be."
Output: "The phone is fast and the screen is good. The aluminum body feels solid, though it picks up fingerprints."

**Example 4 — vague attribution**
Input: "Experts argue that the policy has had a profound impact on the industry."
Output: "A 2024 OECD review found the policy cut compliance costs for small firms by about 12%." (If no real source exists, cut the sentence rather than inventing one.)

## Important cautions

- **Don't invent facts or sources to replace vague ones.** If specificity isn't available, cut the unsupported claim rather than fabricating a concrete-sounding one. Humanizing must not make the text less truthful.
- **None of these signs proves AI authorship on its own** — humans use em dashes, the rule of three, and curly quotes too. The point is to reduce the *cluster* of tells, not to wage war on any single punctuation mark.
- **Detector tools (GPTZero etc.) are unreliable.** Don't optimize for fooling a specific detector; optimize for genuinely natural prose. Good human writing passes for what it is.
- **Keep the user's meaning.** When in doubt about whether something is the author's substantive point or AI decoration, ask, or preserve the substance and strip only the decoration.
