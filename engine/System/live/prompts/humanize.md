# Humanize Text

Take a piece of text and rewrite it so it reads like it was written by a careful human, not generated by a chatbot. The goal is not to "trick a detector" with gimmicks (those break the prose) — it's to remove the genuine stylistic and structural habits that make LLM output recognizable, while keeping the author's meaning, facts, and voice intact.

This skill is built from the patterns catalogued at Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing. The full catalogue with definitions and example word lists lives in `references/ai-tells.md` and `references/word-lists.md` — read those when you need detail on a specific category. The SKILL.md below is enough for most rewrites.

## Core principle

LLM text is recognizable because it "regresses to the mean": it reaches for the most statistically average phrasing, inflates significance, hedges with vague authority, and decorates everything with a fixed set of vocabulary and rhythmic tics. Humanizing means doing the opposite — being specific, plain, and uneven. Real writing has texture: varied sentence length, the occasional blunt short sentence, concrete detail, and no compulsion to editorialize about why something "matters."

Crucially: **do not just swap out flagged words for synonyms.** That leaves the underlying structure intact and can make detection harder rather than producing honest prose. Fix the deeper habit — the inflation, the formula, the hedge — not just its surface vocabulary.

## Workflow

1. **Read the text and identify which tells are present.** Run through the checklist below. Most AI text has a cluster of these, not just one.
2. **Rewrite to remove them**, preserving meaning, facts, citations, and the author's intent. When unsure whether a claim is the author's or an AI embellishment, keep the factual core and cut the editorializing.
3. **Preserve the author's voice and register.** If the original is a casual blog post, don't make it sterile; if it's technical, stay technical. Humanizing is not flattening.
4. **Re-read your output as if hunting for AI tells.** It's easy to introduce new ones (e.g. rule-of-three, an em-dash flourish) while fixing old ones. Do a second pass.
5. **Report what you changed** briefly, so the user understands the edits — unless they asked for just the clean text.

If the user pasted text with no other instruction, default to returning the rewritten text first, then a short note on the main categories of tells you removed.

## The checklist

### Content & framing (the deepest tells — fix these first)

- **Inflated significance / "importance" padding.** LLMs constantly tell the reader how important, notable, enduring, or influential something is. Cut sentences whose only job is to assert significance ("stands as a testament to", "played a pivotal role", "left an indelible mark", "rich cultural heritage"). State the fact; let it carry its own weight.
- **Superficial analysis tacked on.** Watch for trailing present-participle ("-ing") clauses that add hollow commentary: "..., highlighting its importance", "..., reflecting broader trends", "..., cementing its legacy". Delete them or replace with a real, specific observation.
- **Promotional / travel-brochure tone.** Even on neutral topics, LLMs drift into ad copy ("nestled in the heart of", "vibrant", "boasts", "must-visit"). Rewrite to neutral, factual description.
- **Vague attribution / weasel words.** "Observers note", "experts argue", "it is widely regarded", "some critics say" — with no actual source. Either name a real source or cut the claim. Don't attribute opinions to a faceless authority.
- **Canned proof-of-notability.** Listing what kinds of outlets covered something ("received coverage in regional and national media", "profiled in industry publications") as a way of insisting it matters. Cut it or replace with the specific fact.
- **Outline-shaped conclusions.** Formulaic "Despite its challenges... Future prospects..." / "Challenges and Legacy" endings. Drop the rigid scaffold; end where the content naturally ends.
- **Prefer the specific over the generic.** The single biggest humanizing move: replace "a revolutionary titan of industry" with "inventor of the first train-coupling device." Concrete, slightly surprising, true detail is the opposite of AI text.

### Language & grammar

- **AI-vocabulary density.** A cluster of the signature words (delve, underscore, tapestry, intricate, pivotal, crucial, robust, showcase, testament, foster, leverage, garner, multifaceted, "it's worth noting", "in today's world") is one of the strongest tells. Replace with plain equivalents — and reduce the count, don't just substitute. See `references/word-lists.md` for the full list and the era-by-era breakdown.
- **Restore plain "is/are".** LLMs avoid simple copulas, preferring "serves as a", "stands as", "represents", "features", "offers", "boasts". Change "The building serves as the headquarters" to "The building is the headquarters." Use "has" instead of "features/offers/boasts" where it just means has.
- **Negative parallelisms.** "Not just X, but Y", "It's not X, it's Y", "no X, no Y, just Z". Distinctive and overused — rewrite as a plain statement.
- **Rule of three.** Compulsive triples ("fast, reliable, and scalable"; "adjective, adjective, adjective"). Break the pattern: use one or two items, or vary the structure.
- **Elegant variation / forced synonyms.** LLMs avoid repeating a word even when repetition is natural, cycling through synonyms for the same thing. It's fine — often better — to just repeat the plain word.
- **Even rhythm.** AI sentences tend toward uniform medium length. Add variation: a short, blunt sentence next to a longer one. Vary sentence openings.

### Style & formatting

- **Title Case headings** to sentence case unless the house style genuinely requires title case.
- **Overuse of boldface** for emphasis, especially "key takeaways" style bolding of a word every few lines — remove most of it.
- **Inline-header bullet lists** ("**Term:** description" repeated down a list) — convert to prose, or to plain bullets, where a list isn't truly warranted.
- **Em-dash overuse** — LLMs lean on em dashes for punched-up clauses far more than ordinary writers. Replace many with commas, parentheses, periods, or colons. Keep a few if they're genuinely the right call.
- **Curly quotes / apostrophes** appearing in contexts where straight quotes are expected (code, plain text, many web forms) — normalize to straight quotes if that matches the target medium. (Note: curly quotes are correct in professionally typeset prose — judge by destination.)
- **Unnecessary tables** that should be a sentence or two — convert to prose.
- **Skipped heading levels** and **horizontal rules before every heading** — fix to normal document structure.

### Consistency

- **No abrupt style shifts.** If only part of a document was AI-generated, it often reads as a tonal seam against the rest. Make the whole piece consistent in register and rhythm.

## Quick before/after patterns

**Example 1 — significance padding + AI vocabulary**
Input: "Founded in 1923, the company stands as a testament to enduring craftsmanship, playing a pivotal role in shaping the region's vibrant manufacturing landscape."
Output: "The company was founded in 1923 and makes furniture. It was one of the region's larger employers through the 1960s."

**Example 2 — copula avoidance + trailing analysis**
Input: "The museum serves as a cultural hub for the city, offering a diverse array of exhibits that reflect its rich history, underscoring its importance to the community."
Output: "The museum is in the city center and holds about 4,000 objects, mostly local industrial history."

**Example 3 — negative parallelism + rule of three**
Input: "This isn't just a phone — it's a statement. Sleek, powerful, and intuitive, it redefines what a device can be."
Output: "The phone is fast and the screen is good. The aluminum body feels solid, though it picks up fingerprints."

**Example 4 — vague attribution**
Input: "Experts argue that the policy has had a profound impact on the industry."
Output: "A 2024 OECD review found the policy cut compliance costs for small firms by about 12%." (If no real source exists, cut the sentence rather than inventing one.)

## Important cautions

- **Don't invent facts or sources to replace vague ones.** If specificity isn't available, cut the unsupported claim rather than fabricating a concrete-sounding one. Humanizing must not make the text less truthful.
- **None of these signs proves AI authorship on its own** — humans use em dashes, the rule of three, and curly quotes too. The point is to reduce the *cluster* of tells, not to wage war on any single punctuation mark.
- **Detector tools (GPTZero etc.) are unreliable.** Don't optimize for fooling a specific detector; optimize for genuinely natural prose. Good human writing passes for what it is.
- **Keep the user's meaning.** When in doubt about whether something is the author's substantive point or AI decoration, ask, or preserve the substance and strip only the decoration.

# Catalogue of AI-writing signs

Adapted from Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing (WP:AISIGNS), an advice page from WikiProject AI Cleanup. This is a field guide of writing/formatting conventions typical of AI chatbots. Use it to locate and name the specific tell you're rewriting.

Important framing from the source:
- This is **descriptive, not prescriptive** — a list of observations, not rules.
- **Not all text with these features is AI-generated.** LLMs are trained on human writing, so these patterns appear in editorials, blogs, marketing, and fan fiction too.
- Each pattern is a **potential sign**, not proof. The value is in the *cluster*: many tells, many times, in post-2022 text.
- **AI-detection tools (e.g. GPTZero) are unreliable** — don't depend on them.

## 1. Content

LLMs predict the statistically likely next token, so they "regress to the mean": specific, unusual, nuanced facts (statistically rare) get replaced by generic, positive, important-sounding descriptions (statistically common). "Inventor of the first train-coupling device" becomes "a revolutionary titan of industry." This is the root cause of most content-level tells.

**Undue emphasis on significance, legacy, and broader trends.** The text repeatedly asserts how important, influential, enduring, or far-reaching the subject is, rather than just stating facts. It connects the subject to grand themes ("broader movements", "lasting legacy", "shaping generations").

**Canned emphasis on notability, attribution, and media coverage.** The text tries to *prove* the subject is notable by hammering claims of notability — listing the kinds of sources it appeared in (trade publications, regional/national media), echoing the wording of notability guidelines ("independent coverage"), or attributing its own shallow analysis to those sources.

**Superficial analyses.** Shallow commentary about significance/recognition/impact, often via a trailing present-participle ("-ing") phrase at sentence end: "..., highlighting its role", "..., reflecting broader trends." With retrieval-augmented models these may be falsely pinned to a named source regardless of whether the source supports them.

**Promotional and advertisement-like language.** Even when asked for a neutral tone, output drifts toward marketing or travel-guide prose. Subtypes:
- *Cultural-heritage inflation*: constantly reminding the reader of a subject's importance/heritage ("rich cultural heritage", "vibrant", "fascinating glimpse").
- *Brochure framing*: "nestled in the heart of", "must-visit", "gateway to", "seamless".
- Older models (e.g. GPT-4) are more blatantly superlative ("the best"); newer ones are more subtly positive.

**Vague attributions and overgeneralization of opinions.** Weasel wording — attributing claims/opinions to an unnamed authority: "experts argue", "observers note", "it is widely regarded", "researchers and conservationists are interested."

**Outline-like conclusions about challenges and future prospects.** Articles end on a rigid template: a "Challenges" beat followed by "Future Outlook" / "Future Prospects" / "Challenges and Legacy." The tell is the formulaic scaffold, not the mere mention of challenges.

**Leads treating list/broad titles as proper nouns.** In articles whose title is not a proper name (e.g. a list), the first sentence defines the title as if it were a standalone real-world entity, unnaturally.

## 2. Language and grammar

LLM text shows consistent syntactic/word-choice patterns regardless of subject, giving it an identifiable "voice." (Some models vary more than others; GPT-4o produced more syntactic variation than peers.)

**High density of "AI vocabulary".** Studies show LLMs overuse a specific set of words, which spiked in text after 2022 and tend to co-occur. One or two may be coincidence; many, repeatedly, in post-2022 text is one of the strongest single tells. The exact set shifts by model and over time (e.g. "delve" peaked in 2023–early 2024, then fell off). See `word-lists.md` for the words, era breakdown, and phrase lists.

**Avoidance of basic copulatives ("is"/"are").** LLMs replace plain "is/are" with "serves as a", "stands as", "marks", "represents", and prefer marketing verbs ("features", "offers", "boasts") over neutral "has". A documented >10% drop in "is/are" in academic writing appeared in 2023. In leads, they may write "refers to" as if the article were about the term rather than the subject. (Exception: "has" as a past-participle auxiliary is normal.)

**Negative parallelisms.** Constructions that set up a contrast as if correcting a misconception:
- *Not just X, but also Y* — "Not only ... but ...", "It's not just ..., it's ..."
- *Not X, but Y* — "It's not ..., it's ...", "no ..., no ..., just ..." (states the subject lacks the first trait entirely).
Common in human "myth-busting" listicles too, but stereotypically an AI sign.

**Rule of three.** Overuse of triples: "adjective, adjective, adjective" or "short phrase, short phrase, and short phrase." Often used to make a shallow analysis feel comprehensive.

**Lexical diversity / elegant variation.** A repetition penalty pushes models to avoid reusing a word, cycling through synonyms even where plain repetition would read better. (Caveat: if separate AI passages were generated in isolation, this may not show.)

## 3. Style

**Title case** in section headings — capitalizing all main words.

**Overuse of boldface.** Mechanical, excessive bolding for emphasis — often "key takeaways" style, bolding every instance of a chosen term. Inherited from readmes, fan wikis, how-tos, sales pitches, slide decks, listicles. (Some newer models have been instructed to suppress this.)

**Inline-header vertical lists.** Lists where each item is a bolded inline header + colon + description. Bullets may render as raw characters (•, -, –, #, emoji) and numbered lists as literal "1." instead of proper markup; copy-pasting can lose formatting and line breaks.

**Overuse of em dashes.** LLMs use em dashes (—) more than nonprofessional human writing of the same genre, often in a formulaic, "punched-up" sales-like way, where humans would use commas, parentheses, colons, or hyphens. Best used as a tell in combination with others; more common on discussion pages than article text. (Some newer models, e.g. GPT-5.1, were tuned to suppress them.)

**Unusual use of tables.** Occasionally creating small unnecessary tables for content better expressed as prose or an infobox.

**Curly quotation marks and apostrophes.** ChatGPT and DeepSeek often emit curly quotes (" " ' ') instead of straight ones, sometimes mixing both inconsistently. NOT proof on its own: Chicago style, MS Word/macOS "smart quotes", grammar tools, and major newspapers all produce curly quotes legitimately.

**Skipping heading levels.** Jumping heading levels (e.g. straight to a lower level, skipping level 2).

**Thematic breaks before headings.** Inserting a horizontal rule before headings as a matter of habit.

---

## 4. Consistency / miscellaneous

**Pronounced shift in writing style.** When only part of a document is AI-generated, that section reads as a tonal/rhythmic seam against the human-written rest.

Other source-listed miscellaneous signs (mostly Wikipedia-workflow-specific, less relevant to general prose): overwhelmingly exhaustive edit summaries; "submission statements" in drafts; pre-placed maintenance templates; permissions gaming; broken wikitext and reference-markup bugs (e.g. stray "contentReference", "oaicite", "turn0search0" artifacts); fabricated/placeholder citations, invalid DOIs/ISBNs, and book citations lacking page numbers.

---

## 5. Indicators specific to comments and discussion

In discussion/comment contexts, additional AI tells include canned emphasis on quality, good faith, and adherence to policies/guidelines, and the same negative-parallelism and rule-of-three habits showing up in argumentation. Em dashes are especially common here. Treat these the same way: reduce the cluster, restore a plainer, more direct voice.

---

## Note on use

When humanizing, fix the **underlying habit** (inflation, formula, hedge, mechanical formatting), not merely the surface vocabulary. Simply swapping flagged words for synonyms leaves the structure intact and can make the text harder to read without making it more human. And never trade truthfulness for naturalness — cut an unsupported claim rather than inventing a concrete-sounding replacement.


# AI vocabulary and phrase watchlists

From Wikipedia:Signs of AI writing. Use these to (a) spot a tell-cluster and (b) find plainer replacements. A word here is not banned — it's a flag. Judge by density and context: a single "crucial" is nothing; a paragraph stacked with delve/underscore/tapestry/pivotal/robust is a strong tell. Fix the habit, not just the word.

## Master list of overused words ("words to watch")

Additionally (esp. starting a sentence), align with, boasts (meaning "has"), bolstered, crucial, delve, emphasizing, enduring, enhance, fostering, garner, highlight (as a verb), interplay, intricate / intricacies, key (as an adjective), landscape (as an abstract noun), meticulous / meticulously, pivotal, robust, showcase, tapestry (as an abstract noun), testament, underscore (as a verb), valuable, vibrant.

Also commonly cited elsewhere and worth watching: leverage, multifaceted, seamless, realm, navigate (figuratively), foster, cultivate, resonate, profound, groundbreaking, renowned, nestled, "rich history", "diverse array", "stands as", "plays a vital/significant role", "it's worth noting", "in today's world / digital age", "when it comes to", "a testament to", "the world of".

### Plain replacements (illustrative)
- delve into → look at, examine, go into
- underscore / highlight / emphasize → show, point to (often just delete the clause)
- pivotal / crucial / vital / key → important, main, or cut
- robust → strong, solid, reliable
- leverage → use
- foster / cultivate → encourage, build, support
- showcase → show, display
- tapestry / landscape / realm (abstract) → usually delete or name the actual thing
- boasts / features / offers → has
- seamless → smooth, or cut
- a testament to → shows, or cut
- navigate (figurative) → deal with, handle

## Era breakdown (rough, not hard cutoffs)

The vocabulary distribution differs by model and has drifted over time. Use this only to gauge whether text reads as "earlier" or "later" LLM output.

- **Earlier era (≈2023–early 2024):** heavy figurative "delve", "underscore", "tapestry", "testament", "intricate", "vibrant", "rich", lots of em-dash flourishes and blatant superlatives. ("delve" famously peaked here, then dropped sharply through 2025.)
- **Later era (≈2025+):** more subtle positivity, fewer obvious superlatives, more canned notability/attribution language ("independent coverage", "profiled in"), often suppressed em dashes and reduced boldface in models tuned against those habits.

Keep context in mind: e.g. "underscore" can legitimately mean a literal underline or incidental music; "landscape" can be literal terrain. Flag the *figurative/abstract* uses.

## Content-specific phrase watchlists

These bundles tend to appear together within particular content tells.

**Significance / legacy padding:** stands / serves as; is a testament / reminder; a vital / significant / crucial / pivotal / key role / moment; underscores / highlights its importance / significance; reflects broader; symbolizing its ongoing / enduring / lasting; contributing to the; setting the stage for; marking / shaping the; represents / marks a shift; key turning point; evolving landscape; focal point; indelible mark; deeply rooted.

**Canned notability / media coverage:** independent coverage; local / regional / national / [country] media outlets; music / business / tech outlets; profiled in; written by a leading expert; active social media presence.

**Superficial trailing analysis:** highlighting / underscoring / emphasizing …; ensuring …; reflecting / symbolizing …; contributing to …; cultivating / fostering …; encompassing …; valuable insights; align / resonate with.

**Promotional / brochure:** boasts a; vibrant; rich; profound; enhancing; showcasing; exemplifies; commitment to; natural beauty; nestled; in the heart of; groundbreaking; renowned; featuring; diverse array.

**Vague attribution:** industry reports; observers have cited; experts argue; some critics argue; "several sources / publications" (when few or none are actually cited); "such as" before an exhaustive list.

**Outline-shaped conclusion:** "Despite its … faces several challenges …"; "Despite these challenges …"; "Challenges and Legacy"; "Future Outlook"; "Future Prospects".

**Copula avoidance (replace with is/are/has):** serves as / stands as / marks / represents [a]; boasts / features / maintains / offers [a]; refers to.

## How to use this file

1. Scan the target text against the master list and the relevant phrase bundle.
2. If you find a cluster, that confirms the tell — now fix the *structure*, not just the words (see `ai-tells.md` and the SKILL checklist).
3. Replace figurative AI vocabulary with plain equivalents, and reduce the overall count.
4. Never fabricate specifics to replace a vague phrase; cut the unsupported claim instead.
